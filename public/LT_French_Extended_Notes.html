Grammar & structure
French nouns are either masculine or feminine — but endings are reliable guides. The -ion ending is 100% reliable: every French noun ending in -ion is feminine, without exception. When a noun starts with a vowel sound, both le and la shorten to l' — the gender is hidden but still there. To check it, swap in an adjective: l'opinion brillante (fem.) vs. l'ami brillant (masc.).

Object pronouns (le, la = it) always go before the verb they belong to. Je le veux = I it want. This rule never changes, no matter how long the sentence gets. And notice: the same word that means "the" also means "it" — le café → je le veux (the coffee → I want it). French makes this identity explicit where English hides it.

Grammar & structure
The -ation → -er rule works because of a structural parallel: Latin first-conjugation nouns (-ationem) directly correspond to first-conjugation verbs (-are). French simplified both. The rule works when a clear verb exists: célébration → célébrer. But "nation" has no direct verb *natier. The practical test: does the English word have a clear verb? If yes, you can probably find the French verb this way.

Un peu (a little): notice that un and peu stay as two separate words — French keeps them apart where English merges them into "a little." This separation lets you later say un peu de gâteau (a little of cake) — the de connects the quantity to the thing. The N in un is swallowed into the vowel: the same nasal phenomenon you see in -ion words, -on syllables, and -en syllables throughout French.

Grammar & structure
Je peux (I can) and je veux (I want) are modal verbs — they express possibility or desire and are always followed by an infinitive. This is identical to English: "I can swim," "I want to swim." In French the "to" is built into the infinitive ending, so there's no separate word: je peux réserver = I can [to-]reserve.

The COM-/CON- prefix (from Latin cum = with/together) is one of the most productive building blocks in both French and English. Notice it in com-mander (order together), con-firmer (make firm together), com-pliquer (fold together). The "mander" root comes from Latin mandare = to put into someone's hand (manus = hand). That's why: com-mander = give a collective charge, re-com-mander = give a charge again, de-mander = give a request downward, man-date = what is given/charged.

Grammar & structure
French has three ways to ask a question, in increasing formality:
1. Intonation only (most informal): Tu veux ?
2. Est-ce que (neutral — comes in Track 31): Est-ce que tu veux ?
3. Inversion (formal/written): Veux-tu ?

The dash in peux-tu, veux-tu shows they are linked. Special case: inverting je with peux gives not *peux-je but the archaic puis-je — an old form preserved only for this inversion (Track 16).

Latin prefixes seen so far: RE-/RÉ- = again, back (recommander, réparer); COM-/CON- = together (commander, confirmer); DE-/DÉ- = down, from (demander); EX- = out of (explorer, expliquer). These four prefixes alone unlock hundreds of French words.

Grammar & structure
De is the most versatile preposition in French. Its core meanings are "of" and "from," and it shows possession: les clés de la voiture = the keys of the car = the car keys. French doesn't use apostrophe-S — everything goes through de. This pattern (noun + de + noun) builds countless compound concepts.

Plus has two jobs: (1) more in comparatives — plus tard (more late = later), plus grand (more big = bigger); and (2) anymore/no longer in negation — ne...plus, which arrives in Track 35.

The PLIC/PLEX family (from Latin plicare = to fold): complicated = all folded up; explained = un-folded; implicated = folded in; duplicated = two-folded. Even "simple" connects — Latin simplex = one-fold = not complicated. Recognising this root turns each new word into a pattern, not a random sound.

Grammar & structure
Je dois (I must) literally means "I owe" — both senses are alive simultaneously. When you say je dois réparer, you're saying both "I must repair" and "I owe [it] to repair." English works the same way: "I owe it to them to fix this." The debt metaphor for obligation is buried in both languages.

The OI combination gives /wha/ in French: dois, voir, voiture, moi, toi. This is consistent and reliable — whenever you see OI in French, expect /wha/. You already see it in English borrowings: "reservoir," "memoir," "connoisseur."

Words ending in a silent -E always signal that the consonant before it IS pronounced: la voiture (pronounce the R), trente (pronounce the T), la porte (pronounce the T). Without the E, that consonant would be silent — le port (silent T). The E acts as an activator for the preceding consonant.

Grammar & structure
The accent grave (è) on the E signals the open /eh/ sound: réserver, réparer, célébrer. Without the accent, the E gives the muffled /uh/ sound: je, le, recommander. If you hear a clear /eh/, look for the accent. If you hear /uh/, the E has no accent.

The nasal syllable rule applies whenever N or M appears at the END of a syllable. The vowel before gets "swallowed into the nose": AN/AM → /uh/ (manger, ambassadeur), EN/EM → /uh/ (temps, maintenant), IN/IM → /uh/ (inviter, important), ON/OM → /oh/ (confirmer), UN/UM → nasal U (un, parfum).

Maintenant decoded: MAIN-TE-NANT. Three syllables, two nasal endings (MAIN, NANT), and a middle E that disappears in fast speech. Literally: "maintaining in the present" — maintenir (to maintain) + present tense sense. The connection to "maintain" locks the pronunciation in memory.

Grammar & structure
The -ette ending is a diminutive — it originally meant "small [thing]." A baguette was a small stick; a cigarette was a small cigar (le cigare); a bicyclette was a small vehicle. The diminutive meaning faded but the feminine gender stuck. This is why all -ette words are feminine.

Object pronoun position with two verbs: when you have two verbs together (want + to repair, must + to prepare), the pronoun goes before the SECOND verb — the one actually doing the action: je veux le réparer (I want it to repair), je dois la préparer (I must it prepare). This rule is consistent throughout French.

The l' contraction happens with both articles AND pronouns: l'organisation (the organisation), l'organiser (to organise it), l'inviter (to invite him/her). Both follow the same pattern — any vowel-starting word after le or la triggers the contraction.

Grammar & structure

Adjective placement: in French, most adjectives come AFTER the noun. Le café noir (the coffee black), film noir (film black). A handful of very common short adjectives (grand, petit, bon, beau) come before — but until you learn those, after is always safe.

Liaison across word boundaries: the N in un normally disappears (un peu → /uh peu/), but when followed by a vowel, it "jumps" to start the next syllable: un_appartement, un_ami. The N moves position rather than gets stronger. This is why mon_ami sounds like mo-na-mi, not mon-ami.

Grammar & structure
The going-to future with aller: je vais + infinitif = I'm going to + verb. French uses this for planned or imminent future, exactly as English does. Aller is highly irregular in its singular forms because they come from a completely different Latin verb that merged with aller: vais, vas, va come from Latin vadere (to go urgently), while allons, allez, vont come from Latin ambulare (to walk). "Ambulance" comes from ambulare — a walking hospital.

Present for future: you can also use the present tense for near future when context makes it clear: je le fais demain (I'm doing it tomorrow). French uses this more freely than English.

Lundi (Monday = moon day): French day names follow the Latin planetary pattern — lundi (moon), mardi (Mars), mercredi (Mercury), jeudi (Jupiter), vendredi (Venus), samedi (Sabbath), dimanche (Lord's Day). Knowing la lune = moon unlocks lundi and les lunettes.

Grammar & structure
The passé composé with avoir: French most commonly expresses the past as "I have [verb]ed" — avoir + past participle. For ER verbs, the past participle removes -ER and adds -É: manger → mangé, réserver → réservé. The É sounds the same as the infinitive -ER ending — but carries different meaning. The accent distinguishes them in writing.

Avoir (to have) and aller (to go) share their irregularities: j'ai / je vais, tu as / tu vas, il a / il va, ils ont / ils vont. In each pair, avoir is just aller minus the V. Both verbs were used to build the same grammatical categories (past with avoir, future with aller).

Pas encore (not yet = not again): encore normally means "again/still." With ne...pas, it becomes "not yet" — because "not again" implies "not this time yet." French builds complex meanings by combining simple words in logical ways.

Grammar & structure

Past participle as adjective: after être (to be), the past participle agrees in gender and number like any adjective: je suis perdu (masc.), je suis perdue (fem.), elle est vendue (it is sold, fem.). This is exactly how English works: "I am lost" uses the past participle "lost" as an adjective after "to be."

The full present tense picture you now have: I/he/she: ER → minus -ER; RE → minus -RE and preceding consonant silent. Tu: ER → -ES; RE → -S. Nous: ER & RE → -ONS (/oh/).
